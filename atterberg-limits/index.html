Technical details of the service in Fredericton

Fredericton's development history follows the river terraces. Older neighborhoods like Downtown and the University of New Brunswick campus sit on relatively competent glacial till. But the newer commercial strips along Prospect Street and the subdivisions pushing into the former floodplain encounter a different beast: the Leda-type clays and laminated silts deposited by glacial Lake Acadia. These soils can exhibit plasticity indices ranging from 10 to over 40, which changes everything for excavation stability and bearing capacity. Our laboratory procedure follows ASTM D4318-17e1 precisely. We use the Casagrande cup device for the liquid limit determination, counting drops until the groove closes at 13 mm. The plastic limit is determined by hand-rolling threads to 3.2 mm diameter. Every technician in our Fredericton facility calibrates their technique against reference samples quarterly. What makes this test valuable for local projects is its direct input into the Casagrande plasticity chart. When we plot liquid limit against plasticity index, the point falls on a specific zone that tells the design engineer whether the clay is low-plasticity (CL), high-plasticity (CH), or silt-dominated (ML/MH). That classification drives decisions on allowable bearing pressure, swell potential, and drainage requirements.
  • Liquid limit (LL) via Casagrande cup: moisture content at 25 drops
  • Plastic limit (PL): moisture content at 3.2 mm thread crumbling
  • Plasticity index (PI = LL - PL): range indicating clay activity
  • Liquidity index for in-situ consistency assessment
  • Activity ratio (PI / % clay fraction) for swell-shrink prediction

Typical technical challenges in Fredericton

We see the same pattern every spring in Fredericton. A building owner notices cracks in the drywall. The sidewalk tilts toward the street. The culprit is usually a medium to high-plasticity clay that went uncharacterized during design. When Atterberg limits are skipped, the foundation concrete gets poured on soil that will shrink 40 mm during a dry August and swell 30 mm after the November rains. The Saint John River valley has microclimates that intensify these cycles. South-facing slopes dry faster. Low-lying lots near Killarney Lake retain moisture longer. A simple liquid limit and plastic limit test costs a fraction of what crack repairs and underpinning will cost five years later. The numbers do not lie. If your clay plots above the A-line on the Casagrande chart with a PI over 20, you need to design for volume change. No shortcuts. The NBCC references these soil properties in Section 9.12 for foundation requirements, and local building officials expect to see the classification in the geotechnical submission.

What soil types in Fredericton require Atterberg testing?

Any cohesive soil with visible fines content should be tested. In Fredericton, the glacial lake clays along the Saint John River, the laminated silts in the floodplain, and even some weathered till deposits can exhibit plasticity. If the soil can be rolled into a thread when moist, it has plasticity and the Atterberg limits will provide the classification parameters needed for foundation and earthworks design.

How long does the lab take to report results?

Standard turnaround is three to four business days from sample receipt. The drying and wet-preparation stages require time per ASTM D4318, and we run two determinations for each limit to verify repeatability.
