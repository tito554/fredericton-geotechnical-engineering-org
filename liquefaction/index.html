The 2015 National Building Code of Canada places Fredericton in a moderate seismic zone, yet the specific geomorphology of the Saint John River valley introduces a heightened risk that structural engineers cannot afford to overlook. The city's downtown core and the expansive industrial parks along Alison Boulevard sit atop thick sequences of water-saturated alluvial sands deposited during the post-glacial retreat. When these loose, saturated deposits are subjected to the cyclic loading of a seismic event, the pore water pressure can spike faster than the soil can dissipate it, triggering a rapid loss of shear strength. Our liquefaction analysis quantifies this threat by integrating site-specific SPT blow counts, grain size distributions from sonic borings, and groundwater monitoring data to calculate a reliable factor of safety against the onset of flow failure.

A saturated loose sand with a corrected SPT blow count below 15 can transition from a solid to a fluid-like state within seconds during a design-level earthquake.

Technical details of the service in Fredericton

A recent project on a commercial site near the Barker's Point industrial area highlighted the critical nature of this evaluation. The subsurface profile revealed a 4-meter thick layer of loose silty sand with fines content hovering around 15%, directly underlain by the water table at a depth of only 2.5 meters. By applying the NCEER/Youd-Idriss simplified procedure to the corrected SPT-N values, the initial factor of safety against liquefaction dropped below 1.0 for a design earthquake of magnitude 6.0. This prompted a redesign of the foundation system, shifting from conventional footings to a ground improvement strategy using stone columns to densify the susceptible layer and provide drainage paths. For deeper profiles where continuous stratigraphy is essential, we combine the SPT data with CPT testing to resolve thin liquefiable seams that standard penetration tests might miss, ensuring the mitigation design is not based on an averaged or overly optimistic profile.

Typical technical challenges in Fredericton

The dramatic seasonal fluctuation of the Saint John River creates a dynamic groundwater regime that directly amplifies liquefaction risk across Fredericton's floodplain. During the spring freshet, the river can rise by several meters, saturating the upper sand strata and completely eliminating the capillary fringe that normally provides a buffer against pore pressure buildup. This means that a seismic event occurring in April or May, when the river is at its peak discharge, would find the soil column in its most vulnerable state—fully saturated at the surface with zero effective stress in the near-surface layers. The risk extends beyond structural collapse to include lateral spreading toward the riverbanks, which could fracture the underground utility corridors serving the city's north side. A detailed liquefaction analysis must therefore model not just the mean annual groundwater condition, but the worst-case high-water scenario to ensure the post-earthquake serviceability of critical infrastructure.

Our services

The methodology follows a phased approach to progressively reduce uncertainty, moving from preliminary screening to advanced numerical modeling specific to the Fredericton subsurface.

Cyclic Stress Ratio (CSR) Calculation

We determine the seismic demand on the soil by integrating the site-specific peak ground acceleration, total and effective overburden stresses, and a depth-dependent stress reduction coefficient as defined by the NCEER protocols.

Cyclic Resistance Ratio (CRR) Evaluation

The soil's capacity to resist liquefaction is derived from corrected in-situ test data. We apply corrections for overburden pressure, hammer energy ratio, and fines content to the raw SPT-N values before entering the CRR curve.

Post-Liquefaction Settlement Analysis

Using the Tokimatsu & Seed volumetric strain method, we estimate the reconsolidation settlement that occurs as excess pore pressures dissipate, providing a critical input parameter for differential settlement tolerance.

Lateral Spreading Hazard Assessment

For sites adjacent to the Saint John River or its tributaries, we compute the expected lateral displacement using empirically calibrated models that account for the free-face geometry and sloping ground conditions.

Frequently asked questions

Does the NBCC require a liquefaction analysis for standard building permits in Fredericton?

The NBCC 2015 mandates a seismic site classification (Site Class A through E) based on the upper 30 meters of the subsurface. If the investigation identifies loose, saturated sands below the water table and the site class requires a site-specific response spectrum, a liquefaction analysis becomes a code requirement to justify the chosen foundation design and to rule out a Site Class F classification for liquefiable soils.

Can the silty sands along the river actually liquefy, or is the fines content too high?

Silty sands with fines content between 10% and 35% are actually the most susceptible to liquefaction because the silt matrix disrupts grain-to-grain contact without providing the cohesion of true clay. The sands beneath the Fredericton floodplain often fall into this range. Our lab testing measures the Atterberg limits to confirm the non-plastic nature of the fines, which is the critical distinction between a liquefiable silt and a non-liquefiable clay. More info.
