A common mistake in Fredericton is treating a deep excavation like a standard basement dig. The city sits on the floodplain of the Wolastoq (St. John River), where interbedded silts, sands, and soft clays extend well below the surface. When a contractor underestimates lateral earth pressures in these deposits, the shoring system fails before the structural concrete is even poured. We see it most often along Bishop Drive and the downtown waterfront, where the water table sits just two to four meters below grade. A proper deep excavation design must account for transient seepage forces and the low shear strength of the compressible clay layers that dominate the valley. Without a site-specific geotechnical model, the risk of base heave and wall deflection increases sharply. Our team has supported projects from the new Justice Building to university expansions, always starting with a rigorous characterization of the soil profile before selecting the support system.

In Fredericton's sensitive clays, excavation stability hinges more on controlling groundwater than on the structural capacity of the wall itself.

Technical details of the service in Fredericton

The surficial geology along the St. John River corridor is dominated by post-glacial marine clays and alluvial sands deposited during the retreat of the Laurentide Ice Sheet. In Fredericton's downtown core, boreholes routinely encounter 8 to 15 meters of soft Leda-type silty clay with undrained shear strengths as low as 25 kPa. This material is highly sensitive; remolding during excavation can cut its strength by 60 percent or more. Deeper deposits transition into dense glacial till and fractured siltstone bedrock, which provides a reliable bearing stratum for soldier pile tips but introduces groundwater recharge paths that complicate dewatering. We apply limit equilibrium and finite element analyses calibrated with local CPT data to model the staged excavation sequence. The interaction between the shoring system and the compressible clay governs the design — often requiring pre-loaded tiebacks or internal bracing to keep wall movements within tolerable limits for adjacent infrastructure. For projects near the riverbank, we also evaluate the influence of fluctuating river levels on pore pressure distribution behind the wall.

Typical technical challenges in Fredericton

The sharp seasonal contrast in New Brunswick adds a layer of complexity to shoring design. Spring freshet raises the Wolastoq by several meters, saturating the floodplain soils and increasing lateral loads on excavation walls. Come winter, frost penetration can reach 1.5 meters or more in exposed silts, altering the near-surface soil stiffness and potentially locking in higher earth pressures behind rigid walls. We have observed cases where a shoring system performed adequately through autumn but developed excessive deflection during a January cold snap simply because the drainage system froze. The second risk is excavation-induced settlement of neighboring structures — many of Fredericton's historic brick buildings along Queen and York streets rest on shallow footings within the influence zone of a deep dig. Our designs incorporate ground loss estimates and trigger levels for monitoring, aligning with the NBCC's performance-based approach to limit state design. Base instability in the soft clay layer remains the most critical failure mode, and we routinely specify toe embedment depths that extend well into the competent till to prevent rotational slip surfaces from daylighting in the excavation floor.

Our services

The scope of a deep excavation design in Fredericton extends beyond selecting a wall section. We provide a coordinated package that addresses the unique challenges of the St. John River valley.

Geotechnical Site Characterization

Integrated program of mud-rotary boreholes, CPT soundings, and laboratory triaxial tests on undisturbed Shelby tube samples to define the strength and compressibility profile of the sensitive clay.

Shoring System Design

Analysis of cantilever, anchored, and braced wall configurations using both beam-on-elastic-foundation methods and 2D finite element models with staged construction simulation.

Dewatering and Seepage Analysis

Design of deep well or wellpoint systems to lower the phreatic surface below the excavation base, including assessment of drawdown-induced settlement in the surrounding block.

Construction-Phase Monitoring Plan

Specification of inclinometer, piezometer, and survey point arrays with threshold values for wall deflection, groundwater change, and adjacent building movement.

What is the biggest geotechnical challenge for excavations in downtown Fredericton?

The sensitive marine clay is the dominant challenge. Its undrained shear strength can drop sharply if disturbed during excavation or if the base is left exposed too long. Combined with a high water table only a few meters down, the risk of base heave and rapid wall movement is real. Our designs always include a factor of safety against basal instability and a construction sequence that limits open-cut time.

Which shoring system works best in the St. John River valley soils?

It depends on the depth and proximity to neighboring structures. For cuts up to about six meters in open areas, a soldier pile and lagging wall with tieback anchors is common. Deeper excavations or those adjacent to sensitive heritage buildings often require secant pile walls or diaphragm walls to control groundwater ingress and minimize ground loss during installation.

How does the river level affect excavation design?

The Wolastoq water level fluctuates significantly with spring snowmelt and fall storms. A rise in river stage translates directly into higher pore pressures in the alluvial sands that underlie much of the downtown. Our seepage models incorporate seasonal high-water data, and we design dewatering systems to handle the worst-case scenario so the excavation remains stable year-round.
