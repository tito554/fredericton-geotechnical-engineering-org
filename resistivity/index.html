The ABEM Terrameter arrives on site in a compact Pelican case, and within fifteen minutes the four-electrode Schlumberger array is stretching across a Fredericton lawn. It is unassuming gear for what it delivers: a vertical profile of apparent resistivity that distinguishes saturated silts from dry sands, clay lenses from fractured sandstone, and weathered till from competent bedrock, all without turning a single cubic metre of soil. In a city where the geology flips from river-terrace gravels near the Saint John River to dense lodgement till on the hill, knowing what lies beneath the topsoil is not academic; it is the difference between a footing that bears on competent material and one that settles into a buried channel fill. The VES method measures bulk resistivity at increasing electrode spacings, each spacing pushing the effective depth of investigation deeper, so the final sounding curve represents a one-dimensional electrical stratigraphy down to 30 or 40 metres. For projects where borehole access is limited, where the water table is shallow, or where you need continuous coverage between existing test pits, the resistivity sounding fills the gap with repeatable, ASTM D6431-compliant data. Combined with a seismic refraction survey the two geophysical datasets cross-validate each other, giving you both velocity layering and electrical-resistivity lithology in a single investigation window.

A resistivity sounding is not a picture of the soil; it is a measurement of how easily current flows through it, and that flow is controlled by water content, clay fraction, and salinity — three variables every Fredericton foundation design needs to account for.

Technical details of the service in Fredericton

Fredericton grew along the river, and that river has been both a transportation corridor and a geotechnical troublemaker. The downtown core sits on a complex stack of post-glacial sediments: rhythmically bedded silts and clays from the former Glacial Lake Acadia, overlain by coarser outwash sands and gravels where the Saint John River reworked the landscape. The result is a subsurface that can change from 5 ohm-metres (saturated marine clay) to over 150 ohm-metres (dry gravel) within a single city block. Vertical electrical soundings handle this lateral variability by being fast to deploy; we can run three or four soundings in a morning, each one probing to a different depth range depending on the maximum AB/2 spacing we select. The raw field data are plotted as a sounding curve, and the inversion software iterates until the calculated model matches the measured curve within a root-mean-square error of five percent or less. Because the method relies on the electrical properties of the pore fluid as much as the matrix, it is sensitive to salinity, which matters near salted roadways and former industrial parcels along Union Street. Our processing workflow includes a temperature correction and a comparison with laboratory resistivity measurements on any available core samples, tightening the correlation between resistivity and lithology for that specific site.

Typical technical challenges in Fredericton

Take two projects: one on Waterloo Row overlooking the river, another in the Bishop Heights subdivision up on the hill. On Waterloo Row the overburden is thick, the water table sits barely two metres down, and the electrical section is dominated by a low-resistivity zone that corresponds to a soft, normally consolidated clay. A VES sounding here will flag that conductive layer immediately, and the geotechnical engineer can specify a pile length that reaches the underlying till. Up in Bishop Heights, the same VES equipment often reads much higher resistivities because the till is dense, overconsolidated, and sitting directly on sandstone bedrock with minimal weathering. The risk profile flips: in one case you are fighting settlement in saturated clays, in the other you are fighting excavation refusal on shallow bedrock. Without a resistivity survey, both sites look like flat, buildable lots on the surface maps. With the VES data, the contractor knows before the excavator bucket touches the ground whether to expect a wet sticky dig or a hard, dry refusal at two metres. Skipping this step on a Fredericton site with known glacial-lake sediments is not a cost-saving move; it is an invitation for a change order that will far exceed the price of the geophysics.

Our services

Every resistivity campaign in Fredericton begins with a desktop review of surficial geology maps published by the New Brunswick Department of Natural Resources, followed by field calibration against at least one known borehole or outcrop. From that baseline we tailor the array geometry and interpretation workflow to the specific question the project is asking.

Depth-to-Bedrock Soundings for Foundation Design

When a proposed building footprint sits on the Carboniferous sandstone that underlies much of the city, the critical unknown is often the thickness and consistency of the overburden. We run multiple VES stations on a grid, invert the apparent-resistivity data using a smooth-model least-squares algorithm, and deliver a contour map of the bedrock surface elevation. This map lets the structural engineer decide whether shallow footings are feasible or whether piles need to be considered, and it does so at a fraction of the cost of a full drilling grid.

Groundwater and Contaminant-Pathway Mapping

In the lower-lying areas near the Saint John River floodplain, the contrast between clean, resistive sand-and-gravel aquifers and conductive, clay-rich aquitards is pronounced on a resistivity section. We use the VES data to trace confining layers, estimate aquifer thickness, and identify preferential flow paths where a dissolved-phase plume might migrate. This information feeds directly into monitoring-well placement and risk-assessment models, and it satisfies the site-characterization requirements under New Brunswick's Clean Environment Act.

Frequently asked questions

How deep can a VES sounding go in the Fredericton area?

Practical depth depends on the maximum electrode spread we can lay out and on the near-surface resistivity. In most Fredericton settings, with a maximum AB/2 spacing of 60 metres, we achieve an effective depth of investigation of roughly 25 to 30 metres. That is sufficient to reach the Carboniferous bedrock across much of the city except in the deepest buried bedrock valleys near the river. If a project requires deeper penetration, we can extend the spread or combine VES with a seismic refraction line that images to 40 or 50 metres in competent rock.

Can you do resistivity surveys in winter when the ground is frozen?

Winter surveys in Fredericton are possible but require careful planning. Frozen ground increases contact resistance at the electrodes, which we manage by using bentonite-gel-filled electrode holes or by deploying stainless-steel stakes with a higher driving force. The resistivity of frozen soil is higher than unfrozen soil, so the interpretation must account for a seasonal shift in the near-surface values. We have run successful VES campaigns in February and March, but the ideal window is late spring through early November when the active layer is thawed and electrode coupling is straightforward.
