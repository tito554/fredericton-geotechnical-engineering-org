A recent three-storey residential project on Waterloo Row required careful evaluation of the underlying terrace sands before any footing dimensions could be finalized. The developer had assumed a uniform bearing stratum, but our test pits revealed interbedded silt lenses at 2.2 meters depth that would have led to differential settlement under conventional strip footings. In Fredericton, where the Saint John River has carved a broad valley filled with post-glacial deposits, shallow foundation design demands more than textbook bearing capacity equations applied to a single borehole log. The city sits at approximately 20 meters above sea level on a mix of dense ablation till, compressible alluvial silts, and occasional bedrock outcrops of Pennsylvanian sandstone. Understanding which layer will actually support the footing—and how it behaves under long-term loading—requires integrating test pits for visual classification with laboratory strength testing on undisturbed samples. Our team runs unconfined compression and direct shear tests on Shelby tube specimens recovered from the proposed bearing elevation, because the difference between a safe design and a costly underpinning job often comes down to a thin, overlooked seam of softer material.

A thin silt seam at bearing elevation can reduce the allowable bearing pressure by half compared to the surrounding till—and you will not see it without continuous sampling or CPT data.

Technical details of the service in Fredericton

Fredericton records an average of 1,100 millimeters of precipitation annually, and combined with freeze-thaw cycles that penetrate up to 1.8 meters depth, the near-surface soils undergo significant seasonal moisture fluctuations that alter their mechanical properties. We have measured undrained shear strengths in the stiff clay till varying by as much as 40 percent between April and September, a factor that directly controls allowable bearing pressures for shallow foundations. The 2015 edition of the National Building Code of Canada, adopted by the Province of New Brunswick, requires a minimum footing depth of 1.2 meters below finished grade for frost protection, but in low-lying areas near the Nashwaak River confluence we consistently recommend 1.5 meters to stay below the desiccated crust and into more competent material. When site conditions suggest variable compressibility, we complement standard bearing capacity analysis with CPT testing to obtain a continuous profile of tip resistance and sleeve friction, which highlights thin weak zones that conventional split-spoon sampling might miss. For projects on the glacial till uplands south of Prospect Street, where bedrock is shallow and boulders are common, we often pair our footing recommendations with slope stability analysis if the excavation faces exceed 2.5 meters and are within a 1H:1V influence zone of the proposed structure.

Typical technical challenges in Fredericton

The NBCC 2015 Commentary I on geotechnical design explicitly notes that bearing capacity failures are rare compared to serviceability failures from excessive settlement, and this distinction is particularly relevant in Fredericton's alluvial floodplain where normally consolidated silts can experience significant long-term consolidation under foundation loads. A shallow footing placed on a thin sand layer overlying 4 meters of soft clay may meet the ultimate bearing capacity check with a factor of safety of 3.0, yet still settle 50 millimeters over 10 years as pore pressures dissipate, cracking partition walls and binding doors. The risk is compounded when adjacent footings bear on different strata—one on till, one on river terrace sand—creating differential movement that even a well-reinforced grade beam cannot fully mitigate. We have investigated several foundation distress cases in the downtown area where pre-construction site investigations relied solely on SPT blow counts without consolidation testing, missing the compressible nature of the underlying silts altogether. A complete shallow foundation design package must include oedometer tests on representative samples from the stress influence zone, which extends to a depth of at least twice the footing width below the bearing surface.

Our services

Our shallow foundation design support in Fredericton covers the full workflow from field investigation to final bearing capacity recommendations, adapted to the specific soil conditions found across the city's varied terrain.

Bearing capacity and settlement analysis

We calculate net allowable bearing pressures using both general shear (Terzaghi-Buisman) and local shear failure criteria, incorporating strength parameters measured in our triaxial and direct shear apparatus on undisturbed till and sand samples. Settlement predictions use the Casagrande method from oedometer curves, with the compression index derived from incremental loading tests at the project-specific stress range.

Footing dimensioning and reinforcement recommendations

Based on the allowable bearing pressure and column loads, we provide preliminary spread footing widths and thicknesses that satisfy both geotechnical (bearing capacity, settlement) and structural (punching shear, flexure) limit states per CSA A23.3. For mat foundations on compressible soils, we work with the structural engineer to model the modulus of subgrade reaction from our consolidation data.

Construction-phase subgrade verification

After excavation reaches the design bearing elevation, we return to the site to perform proof-rolling observations, dynamic cone penetration (DCP) testing, and hand-auger sampling to confirm that the exposed material matches the geotechnical model. Any soft spots or disturbed zones are identified before the mud slab is poured, avoiding post-construction disputes.

How deep do footings need to be in Fredericton to avoid frost heave?

The NBCC 2015 specifies a minimum footing depth of 1.2 meters below finished grade for frost protection in the Fredericton area. However, we routinely recommend 1.5 meters in low-lying zones along the Saint John River and Nashwaak River corridors where the water table is high and the soil remains saturated through the winter, because saturated silts and fine sands are particularly susceptible to ice lens formation and heave.

Can you design shallow foundations on the clay till found in many parts of Fredericton?

Yes, the dense clay till that underlies much of Fredericton's upland neighborhoods provides excellent bearing capacity, with net allowable pressures typically between 150 and 300 kPa. Our laboratory program for till sites includes Atterberg limits, unconfined compression, and consolidated-undrained triaxial tests to define the drained and undrained strength envelopes. The key variable is the presence of sand lenses and perched water, which we map through continuous sampling or CPT soundings to ensure the bearing stratum is uniform under the entire footprint.

What laboratory tests are essential for shallow foundation design?

At minimum, a shallow foundation investigation requires moisture content, Atterberg limits, grain size distribution, and unconfined compression or direct shear tests on samples from the proposed bearing elevation. For sites with soft clay or silt within the stress influence zone—common in Fredericton's river valley—we add one-dimensional consolidation (oedometer) tests to predict settlement magnitude and rate. If groundwater is within the excavation depth, permeability tests help the contractor design dewatering systems.
