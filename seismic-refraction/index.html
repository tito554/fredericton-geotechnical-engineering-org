We still see it now and then: a contractor in Fredericton drills a test hole, hits a few cobbles at 6 meters, and assumes they have reached competent bedrock. Then the excavator arrives and gets stuck in till with boulders the size of a small car. The Saint John River valley has a complicated glacial history that left behind buried channels, discontinuous till layers, and weathered shale contacts that just do not show up clearly on a drill log alone. Seismic tomography, both refraction and reflection, maps the velocity contrast between overburden and intact rock continuously across a site, so you know exactly where the real refusal surface sits before you bid the earthworks. When we combine this with a few test pits for ground-truthing the upper 3 meters, the subsurface picture becomes far sharper than either method alone could produce.

A 300 m/s velocity horizon in Fredericton shale usually means weathered, highly fractured rock that will rip, not blast.

Technical details of the service in Fredericton

A recent project on the south bank of the Nashwaak River involved a proposed four-storey institutional building over interbedded sandstone and siltstone of the Fredericton Formation. The boreholes showed competent rock at 8 to 11 meters, but between two of the boreholes the excavation later revealed a 14-meter-deep trough filled with soft organic silt: an old paleochannel that nobody anticipated. Seismic refraction tomography would have caught that. A low-velocity anomaly right between those two boreholes would have flagged the buried channel before the shovels went in. On the same site we ran a reflection line to image deeper structure for foundation design, and the stacked section showed a clear discontinuity at roughly 30 meters that correlated with a known fault mapped by the New Brunswick Geological Survey. For sites with variable rock quality we often pair the seismic grid with slope stability analysis when the excavation will leave a temporary cut higher than 4 meters.

Typical technical challenges in Fredericton

Much of Fredericton sits on the Pennsylvanian Fredericton Formation: grey sandstone, siltstone, and shale that weathers to a soft, clay-rich saprolite. The depth of weathering can swing from 2 meters to more than 20 meters across a single lot, and that variation directly controls seismic site class under the NBCC. Misclassifying a Site Class C as a Site Class D can inflate design spectral accelerations by 30 percent or more, adding unnecessary steel and concrete to a building that did not need it. On the other hand, assuming rock is shallow everywhere and designing for Site Class B when a low-velocity weathered zone persists below the footings is worse: it under-predicts ground motion in an earthquake. The 2020 NBCC hazard model raised spectral ordinates for eastern Canada, so getting the Vs30 and the deeper velocity profile right is not optional anymore.

Our services

Our Fredericton seismic tomography work covers the practical range of what local geotechnical projects actually need, from shallow refraction for utility corridors to deeper reflection profiling for bridge foundations.

Seismic Refraction Tomography

Ray-tracing inversion of first-arrival travel times to map compressional wave velocity in 2D cross-sections. Used routinely across Fredericton for bedrock surface profiling, rippability assessment, and identifying low-velocity zones within the overburden.

Seismic Reflection Profiling

Common-midpoint (CMP) acquisition and processing to image stratigraphic boundaries and structure below 30 meters. Applied in the Saint John River valley for deep foundation investigations and fault characterization.

Vs30 Determination for NBCC Site Class

Combined refraction and MASW acquisition on the same spread to compute time-averaged shear-wave velocity in the upper 30 meters, directly supporting the Seismic Site Class assignment required by NBCC 2020.

Crosshole Seismic Tomography

High-resolution P- and S-wave velocity imaging between boreholes, reaching sub-meter resolution for critical infrastructure where voids, dissolution features, or subtle lithologic changes must be mapped with precision.

How does seismic tomography help determine the NBCC Seismic Site Class?

The NBCC 2020 classifies a site based on the time-averaged shear-wave velocity in the upper 30 meters, or Vs30. Seismic refraction gives us the P-wave velocity structure; we convert that to shear-wave velocity using site-specific Poisson ratios derived from the local Fredericton Formation lithology, or we run a parallel MASW line to measure Vs directly. Either way, the goal is the same: assign a Site Class from A to E per Table 4.1.8.4.A so the structural engineer uses the correct design spectrum.

Can seismic refraction map the depth to bedrock through the dense glacial till common in the Fredericton area?

Yes, and that is actually the most frequent application we run in the region. The lodgement till deposited during the Wisconsinan glaciation has a P-wave velocity typically between 1200 and 2200 m/s, while the underlying Fredericton Formation sandstone and shale exceed 3000 to 4000 m/s when unweathered. The velocity contrast is strong enough that the refraction method resolves the till-bedrock interface reliably, even when the till contains boulders.

How long does a seismic tomography survey and report take?

Field acquisition for a single refraction line of 115 to 230 meters takes one day with a two-person crew, assuming reasonable access and weather. Processing and tomographic inversion add two to three working days. A signed, stamped report including velocity sections, interpreted geologic profiles, and the Vs30 calculation is typically delivered within five to seven business days after the field work.
